Independent exams of the evidence behind AI claims

The vendor says it works. Who checked?

For insurers writing AI coverage, companies buying AI, and investors doing diligence.

When an AI vendor says its product is safe or accurate, we examine the evidence behind that one claim, as examiners who don't work for the vendor, and sign what it does and doesn't show. The examination

What an examination checks, and what it doesn't

It's one examination, for anyone relying on a specific AI claim: an insurer pricing coverage, a company buying an AI product, or an investor in a deal. You name the claims your decision depends on. For each claim, the report gives two findings.

First, what the vendor's evidence is: missing, shown to be flawed, not enough to judge, sound but short of the claim, or sound and reaching it. Second, whether sound evidence inside the claim's scope shows the claim is false. If none does, that isn't a pass.

What it doesn't do

It doesn't test the product. It doesn't say whether the product works, is safe, is good, or will perform once it's deployed. There's no score, grade, tier or certification. It says nothing about claims you didn't name, beyond listing them. The examiner doesn't fix anything.

What it can't tell you

  • It can't confirm the records are real beyond checking them for consistency.
  • It can't confirm the version tested is the one you'd receive.
  • Zero problems found in a sample means zero found in that sample.

What you bring

The claims you rely on, and the vendor's evidence for each, through you and under your NDA. The examiner needs no source code, running copy of the product, or credentials. If a check needs one of those, the report says unknown and names what it needs.

Our own test

We test our method first, in public

Before we sell an examination, we test the method blind. A red team builds eight made-up vendor cases, two clean and six with planted faults. A human inspector and an AI inspector work them apart. Then an outside examiner repeats the round without the key. Everything is released, pass or fail.

I ask three questions of any check, starting with my own.

Who wrote the list?Not the vendor. Two lists, built apart. A red team plants problems in made-up evidence files, some outside both lists.
Do I pay more for a pass?No. Every fee is set before the work starts, and a refusal pays the same.
Who can overrule the result?Not me. A signer who wrote none of it applies the scoring rules. I can't overrule a refusal.

Eight cases can't give a general error rate. They show whether the method catches what it's built to catch. More rounds come after that.

Who examines

The examiner isn't me

I designed the method, and AI models drafted it. I'm not the examiner. The examiner signs the findings and has to have shown practice checking software or systems against stated criteria: for example, an FAA software designee, an independent verification and validation engineer, or a test and evaluation engineer.

A second reviewer, independent of the examiner, reads every finding and either releases it or sends it back with the point in dispute. Both write down their ties to the vendor, to you and to the deal before the work starts. The vendor can answer written questions and can't edit a finding.

None of this makes the examination a certification. It's a written finding on the vendor's evidence, and on what's still open, for the scope you named.

The market

What exists, and what doesn't

The market today offers deal diligence, certification of how a company manages AI (ISO/IEC 42001), checks the vendor pays for, and checks run by an insurer that also sells the coverage. I haven't found a published method, run by examiners who don't work for the vendor, that checks whether the evidence behind a specific AI claim holds up, tested blind, repeated by an outside examiner, with an error rate.

In buildings, electrical products and aircraft software, the company being checked doesn't write the standard by itself or decide who's qualified to check against it. For AI, standards are starting to form. The rest mostly isn't there yet.

Before you rely on it

Before you rely on a vendor's report, ask who checked it

If you're shown test results for an AI product you're about to insure, buy or fund, ask where they came from. These are the questions I'd ask first.

Who ran the tests, and did the company pay them?

Where did the list of things to test come from? If it came from the product, what isn't on it?

Are results on bad or unusual inputs reported apart from results on normal ones?

Who paid for the evaluation, and does the report say so?

If you run the same input again, do you get the same answer?

Can you read the full report, or only a summary and a badge?
