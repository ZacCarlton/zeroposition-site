Rules already in place

Banks, lenders and brokers can already ask for the evidence

Bank supervisors expect a bank to check the models it buys, not only the ones it builds. These are the rules a bank, lender or broker can point to.

  1. Vendor models at banks. In April 2026 the Federal Reserve, the OCC and the FDIC replaced their 2011 model risk guidance with revised guidance (SR 26-2; OCC Bulletin 2026-13). It holds vendor and third-party models to the same principles as models a bank builds itself, including validation. Generative and agentic AI models are outside its formal scope; the agencies said a request for comment on AI would follow.
  2. Vendors in general. The agencies' 2023 guidance on third-party relationships has a bank do due diligence before it signs and monitor the vendor after. In September 2026 they proposed replacing it with guidance that scales that work to each relationship's risk. Comments closed November 16, 2026.
  3. Credit decisions. Under the Equal Credit Opportunity Act and Regulation B, a lender that denies credit must give the specific reasons, including when a model made the decision.
  1. Broker-dealers. FINRA's 2026 oversight report says its rules on supervision, communications, recordkeeping and fair dealing apply to generative AI. A firm that relies on AI in its supervisory system may consider the integrity, reliability and accuracy of the model, including tools its vendors supply.
  2. State law. Colorado's SB 26-189, signed in May 2026, sets duties for developers and users of automated decision tools in consequential decisions, including financial and lending services, from January 1, 2027. It replaced the state's 2024 AI Act and carries no blanket exemption for financial institutions.
Where it fits

Where an outside check fits work you already do

  1. Vendor due diligence. Before you sign, make the vendor's evidence for each claim you'll rely on a required submittal, and have it examined. See the sample requirement.
  2. Model validation. When you can't see a vendor's model or data well enough to validate it yourself, a written finding on the vendor's own evidence gives your validators something to work from.
  3. In a deal. If you're investing in or buying a company whose value rests on its AI, the check can be a condition of closing. See the page for investors.

You can start with a pilot: a first look at one AI product, for a fixed $9,000.

The claim

"Our fraud model cuts false declines by 30% without letting more fraud through."

Finding 1, how far the evidence reaches: Not enough to judge. The vendor's test ran on one bank's card data for six weeks and doesn't report the fraud it missed, so the second half of the claim can't be judged.

Finding 2, sound evidence the claim is false: None found inside the claim's scope. The open question is the fraud the model let through, and the vendor's evidence doesn't measure it.

Before you rely on an AI claim

You can require an outside check

If your decision rests on what a vendor says its AI product does, you can make an outside check of that claim a condition of the purchase, the policy or the deal. The report comes only to you.
