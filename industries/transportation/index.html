Rules already in place

Carriers, transit agencies and vehicle makers can already ask for the evidence

These are the rules a buyer in aviation, transit or road vehicles can point to when it asks an AI supplier for evidence.

  1. Aircraft. Under FAA Part 21, an applicant shows its design complies and lets the FAA inspect and test, with a separate approval path for major changes. The FAA's 2024 roadmap for AI safety assurance describes how AI would be certified into a design.
  2. Airline safety management. The FAA's safety management rule (Part 5, effective May 28, 2024) has covered operators manage safety risk for new or changed systems and check that their risk controls work.
  3. Transit. FTA's Part 673 has transit agencies run safety risk management and performance monitoring, and larger operators manage change. Under Part 674, state safety oversight agencies run triennial audits of rail transit agencies.
  1. Automated vehicles in California. The DMV's Article 3.8 rules, approved April 28, 2026, call for evidence-backed safety cases, and the DMV's review of a safety case may involve third-party experts.
  2. Federal vehicle rules. Manufacturers certify that vehicles meet federal safety standards (49 U.S.C. 30115). NHTSA encourages a voluntary safety self-assessment for automated driving systems, and it withdrew its proposed AV STEP program on June 26, 2026.
Where it fits

Where an outside check fits work you already do

  1. At supplier selection. Make the supplier's evidence for each claim you'll rely on a required submittal, and have it examined before you accept its design evidence. See the sample requirement.
  2. Before a change approval or a filing. A written finding on the supplier's evidence fits before preproduction testing, a safety-change approval or a regulatory submission.
  3. If you insure the fleet. An underwriter covering a fleet that relies on driver-assist or routing AI can ask for a finding on the claims the cover depends on. See the page for insurers.

The claim

"Our driver-monitoring camera catches 98% of drowsy drivers."

Finding 1, how far the evidence reaches: Not enough to judge. The vendor tested in daylight with drivers acting drowsy on cue. There's no night data and no real fatigue, so the number can't be judged for real routes.

Finding 2, sound evidence the claim is false: None found. The evidence doesn't reach the conditions the claim covers.

Before you rely on an AI claim

You can require an outside check

If your decision rests on what a vendor says its AI product does, you can make an outside check of that claim a condition of the purchase, the policy or the deal. The report comes only to you.
